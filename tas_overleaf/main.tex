\title{Invalid Is Not Failure:\\
Censoring-Aware Evaluation of Long-Horizon AI Agents}

\begin{abstract}
Long-running agents sometimes stop without producing a valid task outcome
because the provider, runtime, or harness failed rather than the agent. Reported
reliability then depends on a reporting choice: count such executions as
failures, or drop them. We show these choices bias in \emph{opposite}
directions, and that the bias is not benign even when infrastructure failures
are independent of agent behaviour. Because a per-step failure hazard compounds
over a trajectory, survival is $s^{H}$ in the horizon $H$, so censoring becomes
informative whenever $H$ is associated with success. We derive
$p_{\mathrm{drop}}-p=\mathrm{Cov}(Y,s^{H})/\mathbb{E}[s^{H}]$ and
$p_{\mathrm{fail}}-p=\mathbb{E}[Y(s^{H}-1)]\le 0$. On 80{,}035 public
software-engineering agent trajectories, where resolved runs average 15.2 agent
steps and unresolved runs 28.7, a 1\% per-step hazard moves reported reliability
from a true 16.73\% to 18.27\% (drop) or 14.46\% (fail): a 3.8-point spread from
the reporting policy alone. Inverse-probability-of-censoring weighting recovers
the true rate to within 0.01 points when the censoring model is known, and
degrades predictably when it is not. We argue reliability reports should
separate semantic failure from censoring and state the censoring mechanism.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A long-horizon agent run can end for two very different reasons. The agent can
fail the task, or the measurement apparatus can fail the agent: a provider quota
is exhausted, an API returns 503, a container dies, a harness times out. The
first is evidence about the agent. The second is not. Yet benchmark reports
usually reduce both to a single success rate, and the reduction happens through
an unstated reporting policy --- either such runs are scored as failures, or
they are dropped from the denominator.

We are not the first to observe that agent benchmarks have validity problems
\citep{kapoor2024agentsthatmatter,weidinger2025evalscience,zhu2026guardrails}.
Our claim is narrower and, we think, underappreciated: \emph{the two obvious
ways of handling invalid runs are biased in opposite directions, and neither is
conservative.} The reason is structural. Suppose infrastructure failures are
entirely independent of what the agent is doing, arriving with a small constant
hazard $q$ at each agent decision step. A run of horizon $H$ then survives with
probability $s^{H}$, $s=1-q$. Long runs are exposed more often. So even a
perfectly outcome-independent per-step failure becomes \emph{informative at the
trajectory level} the moment horizon is associated with success --- which, in
practice, it strongly is: on the corpus we study, resolved trajectories average
15.2 agent steps and unresolved ones 28.7.

This is right-censoring, and the statistical machinery for it is standard
\citep{horvitz1952generalization,robins1994estimation}. The contribution is the
mapping, the direction result, and the magnitude on real agent data.

\vspace{2pt}
\noindent\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep1pt \parskip0pt \topsep2pt
\item \textbf{Formal.} We model infrastructure-invalid executions as censored
outcomes and derive how horizon converts outcome-independent per-step failures
into systematic, opposite-signed reliability bias (Section~\ref{sec:model}).
\item \textbf{Empirical.} On $\approx$80k real software-engineering agent
trajectories we quantify how the two common policies distort aggregate and
model-level reliability across a predeclared hazard grid
(Sections~\ref{sec:setup}--\ref{sec:results}).
\item \textbf{Correction and artifact.} We evaluate censoring-aware estimation
under known and misspecified censoring, and pair it with an instrumented
harness that separates semantic failure from infrastructure invalidation
(Section~\ref{sec:integrity}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Agent Evaluation Under Censoring}
\label{sec:model}

Let $Y_i\in\{0,1\}$ be the true semantic task outcome of execution $i$, $H_i$
its horizon in agent decision steps, and $C_i\in\{0,1\}$ the indicator that the
run stays infrastructure-valid long enough for $Y_i$ to be observed. The
estimand is $p=\mathbb{E}[Y]$. Under a constant per-step invalidation hazard $q$
applied independently of the agent's behaviour, with $s=1-q$,
\begin{equation}
\Pr(C_i=1\mid H_i=h)=s^{h}.
\label{eq:survival}
\end{equation}
We assume $C \perp Y \mid H$ and $\mathbb{E}[s^{H}]>0$.

\paragraph{Proposition 1 (Horizon-induced censoring bias).}
Under \eqref{eq:survival}, the complete-case (drop-invalid) estimand is
$p_{\mathrm{drop}}=\mathbb{E}[Ys^{H}]/\mathbb{E}[s^{H}]$ and
\begin{equation}
p_{\mathrm{drop}}-p=\frac{\mathrm{Cov}(Y,s^{H})}{\mathbb{E}[s^{H}]}.
\label{eq:drop}
\end{equation}
The invalid-as-failure estimand is $p_{\mathrm{fail}}=\mathbb{E}[Ys^{H}]$ and
\begin{equation}
p_{\mathrm{fail}}-p=\mathbb{E}\!\left[Y(s^{H}-1)\right]\le 0 .
\label{eq:fail}
\end{equation}

\noindent\emph{Proof.} $\mathbb{E}[YC]=\mathbb{E}[Y\,\mathbb{E}[C\mid Y,H]]
=\mathbb{E}[Ys^{H}]$ and $\mathbb{E}[C]=\mathbb{E}[s^{H}]$, so
$p_{\mathrm{drop}}=\mathbb{E}[Ys^{H}]/\mathbb{E}[s^{H}]$; subtracting $p$ and
writing $\mathbb{E}[Ys^{H}]-\mathbb{E}[Y]\mathbb{E}[s^{H}]=\mathrm{Cov}(Y,s^{H})$
gives \eqref{eq:drop}. Scoring invalid runs as $0$ gives
$p_{\mathrm{fail}}=\mathbb{E}[YC]=\mathbb{E}[Ys^{H}]$, and $s^{H}\le1$ with
$Y\ge0$ gives \eqref{eq:fail}.\hfill$\square$

The sign of the complete-case bias is therefore governed entirely by the
association between outcome and horizon. Since $s^{H}$ decreases in $H$, if
successful runs are \emph{shorter} then $\mathrm{Cov}(Y,s^{H})>0$ and dropping
invalid runs \emph{overstates} reliability, while invalid-as-failure always
understates it. The two policies bracket the truth from opposite sides, and the
gap widens with $q$.

\paragraph{Censoring-aware estimation.}
With $\pi_i=\Pr(C_i=1\mid H_i)=s^{H_i}$ known, the Horvitz--Thompson estimator
$\hat p_{\mathrm{HT}}=n^{-1}\sum_i C_iY_i/\pi_i$ is unbiased, since
$\mathbb{E}[C_iY_i/\pi_i]=\mathbb{E}[Y_i]$. The H\'ajek (stabilized) form
$\hat p_{\mathrm{H}}=\sum_i C_iY_i/\pi_i \big/ \sum_i C_i/\pi_i$ is a ratio
estimator: consistent, not exactly unbiased. Both degrade as $\pi_i\to0$ for
long horizons, a positivity problem that no weighting fixes.

\paragraph{Scope.} These results assume independent per-step censoring with a
known hazard. They do not cover correlated outages, adaptive provider quotas,
retry policies, task-dependent infrastructure, multi-attempt inference, or
unknown censoring mechanisms.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Data.} We use a public corpus of 80{,}036 SWE-agent trajectories
\citep{nebius2025trajectories} (CC-BY-4.0), each with a ground-truth resolution
label from benchmark test execution. We read only five pruned columns
($\approx$0.6\,MB of a 1.11\,GB corpus). One row has no parsed agent step,
leaving $n=80{,}035$. We do \emph{not} filter on the dataset's own
\texttt{exit\_status}: its categories are strongly associated with success
(resolve rate 0.248 for \texttt{submitted} vs.\ 0.000 for \texttt{exit\_context}),
so filtering on it would condition on the outcome. Those statuses are part of
the historical generating process, not our censoring.

\paragraph{Horizon.} $H$ is the number of trajectory messages authored by the
agent (\texttt{role="ai"}), i.e.\ agent decision turns; scaffold and environment
turns are excluded. The definition was fixed before any censoring result was
computed. We re-fetched 20 randomly chosen full trajectories (seed 20260905) and
recounted independently: \textbf{20/20 exact agreement}. Our counts are
deliberately about half the dataset card's step figures, which count both agent
and environment messages; $2H{+}1$ reproduces the card exactly.

\paragraph{Censoring is semi-synthetic.} We inject censoring on top of this
completed, labelled population. The hazard grid
$q\in\{0,0.10,0.25,0.50,1.00,2.00\}\%$ per agent step was predeclared. It is a
\textbf{sensitivity analysis}, not a measured outage rate for any provider.
Because every trajectory has observed $Y$ and $H$, $p_{\mathrm{drop}}$ and
$p_{\mathrm{fail}}$ are computed \emph{analytically}; Monte Carlo (200
replicates, seeds derived from 20260905) is used only to validate the
estimators.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\paragraph{The population is strongly horizon-asymmetric.}
True reliability is $p=16.73\%$ (13{,}389 of 80{,}035). Resolved trajectories
average $15.17$ agent steps (median 12); unresolved average $28.70$ (median 19).
Success is markedly shorter, so Proposition~1 predicts $\mathrm{Cov}(Y,s^{H})>0$
and an upward complete-case bias --- which is what we observe.

\paragraph{The reporting policy alone moves reliability by several points.}
Table~\ref{tab:analytic} and Figure~\ref{fig:main} show the divergence.
At a mild $q=0.5\%$ the two policies differ by 2.04 points on a 16.73\% base
(a 12\% relative spread); at $q=2\%$ they differ by 6.82 points (41\% relative).
Complete-case analysis overstates and invalid-as-failure understates, exactly as
\eqref{eq:drop}--\eqref{eq:fail} require.

\paragraph{The mechanism is visible in horizon strata.}
Trajectories of 1--25 steps are 65.3\% of the corpus and succeed at 23.0\%;
those beyond 75 steps are 4.4\% and succeed at 2.6\%. At $q=2\%$ the short
stratum retains 77.6\% of runs but the long stratum only 10.5\%. Censoring
preferentially deletes the long, low-success runs, which is precisely why the
survivors look better than the population.

\paragraph{IPCW recovers the estimand when the mechanism is known.}
Across 200 Monte Carlo replicates per hazard, Horvitz--Thompson bias never
exceeds $0.007$ points, versus $+1.54$ and $-2.27$ points for the two policies
at $q=1\%$. The cost is variance: HT standard deviation grows from $0.016$ to
$0.096$ points as $q$ goes from $0.1\%$ to $2\%$, and the H\'ajek form grows
faster ($0.125$ points at $q=2\%$) as some weights become large.

\paragraph{Rankings did not reverse; effect sizes did distort.}
Three models clear the predeclared $\geq$500-trajectory threshold. Across all
six hazards and both policies we observed \textbf{zero pairwise ranking
reversals} (3 pairs; Spearman $\rho=1.000$ throughout). We report this null
result plainly: with three well-separated models (25.9\%, 16.7\%, 15.2\%) this
is a weak test of ranking stability, and we do not claim rankings are generally
safe. What does move is the apparent \emph{gap}: the 405b--8b difference is
$10.71$ points truly, $15.01$ points under drop-invalid at $q=2\%$ (inflated
$\approx$40\%) and $8.43$ points under invalid-as-failure (compressed
$\approx$21\%). Comparative claims about margins are distorted even where
ordering survives.

\paragraph{Correction depends on modelling the mechanism.}
We censored under a hazard that doubles beyond step 25 while weighting under a
constant-hazard approximation. HT bias moves from $\approx$0 to $-0.14$ points
($q_0=0.5\%$) and $-0.26$ ($q_0=1\%$); H\'ajek to $+0.55$ and $+0.97$. The
correction degrades gracefully here, but it degrades: IPCW does not rescue
unknown or outcome-dependent censoring.

\section{From Censoring to Measurement Integrity}
\label{sec:integrity}

Censoring is only estimable if the harness records it. In our own instrumented
runs it was not hypothetical: two provider-backed agent executions completed 9
and 4 agent steps before rate-limit invalidation, and six continuations restored
from checkpoints were invalidated before executing any step. No valid benchmark
outcome existed for any of the eight, and our harness assigned none. We report
this only to establish that infrastructure invalidation occurs; the large-scale
study above is semi-synthetic and does \emph{not} use this event frequency as
$q$, nor does it estimate any provider's reliability.

Making that distinction routine requires separating channels that harnesses
commonly merge. Our artifact enforces, as regression tests, that online
behavioural features see only a length-$k$ trajectory prefix and never the
outcome; that provider events never increment semantic failure counters; that
the evaluator runs in isolated state with the official test patch; that
repository-change features are repository-scoped rather than container-wide; and
that raw artifacts are immutable and hashed. Table~\ref{tab:invalidity} lists
the invalidity sources we have controlled demonstrations for.

\paragraph{Minimum reporting.} We suggest agent reliability reports state, at
minimum: validity status and invalidation reason per run; the censoring or
retry mechanism and its rate; the horizon distribution by outcome; and which
invalid-run policy produced the headline number. These are a starting point for
discussion, not a standard.

\section{Related Work}

Where prior work studies how agents fail, we study which executions may be
counted as evidence at all. \citet{zhao2026failure} anatomise coding-agent
failure as a process over complete trajectories; \citet{ruan2026doomed} abort
doomed episodes early --- an intervention that is itself a censoring mechanism
under our framing, and one whose hazard would need reporting;
\citet{li2026early} derive online warning signals for wasted computation and
encounter, but do not model, a missing-outcome population.
\citet{gonuguntla2026replay} show static replay under a substituted model scores
a counterfactual world; that concerns procedure validity rather than missing
outcomes. Two recent papers use adjacent vocabulary with different content:
\citet{wang2026interface} show a serving interface can suppress tool calls
before evaluation, where ``censoring'' means output suppression rather than a
missing-data mechanism, and \citet{raghu2026proper} extend proper scoring rules
to administratively censored trajectories --- the closest work, though it scores
predictions rather than estimating an aggregate benchmark parameter, and uses no
inverse-probability weighting. On validity more broadly,
\citet{kapoor2024agentsthatmatter}, \citet{weidinger2025evalscience},
\citet{zhu2026guardrails} and \citet{chen2026judge} motivate rigorous evaluation
constructs. Our estimators are classical
\citep{horvitz1952generalization,robins1994estimation}; the contribution is the
mapping onto agent benchmarks, the horizon-covariance result, and its magnitude
on real trajectories. SWE-bench \citep{jimenez2024swebench,
openai2024swebenchverified} and SWE-agent \citep{yang2024sweagent} supply the
benchmark and scaffold lineage of our corpus. We do not claim to be first.

\section{Limitations}

(1) The censoring study is \textbf{semi-synthetic}: we inject censoring into a
completed corpus rather than observing real outages. (2) The $q$ grid is a
sensitivity analysis, not a measured provider outage rate. (3) The primary
model assumes independent, constant per-step censoring; the stress test probes
only one departure. (4) IPCW requires a correct and estimable censoring model,
which real harnesses would have to fit rather than know. (5) Positivity and
variance degrade for long horizons and high $q$. (6) Results come from one
public software-engineering corpus with one scaffold family and three models;
the ranking analysis has only three eligible models and is a weak test. (7) The
real provider case demonstrates existence, not prevalence, and yields no
reliability estimate. (8) Replication on other agent domains, scaffolds, and
genuinely observed infrastructure failures remains future work.

\section{Conclusion}

An invalid execution is not a failed one. We showed that treating it as either a
failure or a missing row biases reported agent reliability in opposite
directions, that the bias arises from horizon compounding even when
infrastructure failures are outcome-independent, and that on 80{,}035 real
agent trajectories the reporting policy alone can move reported reliability by
several points. Censoring-aware estimation recovers the intended estimand when
the mechanism is known and degrades predictably when it is not. The practical
implication is modest and concrete: agent evaluations should record validity per
run, report the censoring mechanism, and say which policy produced the number.
