\begin{abstract}
Long-running agents sometimes stop without a valid task outcome because the
provider, runtime, or harness failed rather than the agent. Reported reliability
then depends on a reporting choice: score such runs as failures, or drop them.
We show these choices err in \emph{opposite} directions, and that the error
persists even when infrastructure failures are independent of agent behaviour: a
per-step hazard compounds to an observation probability $s^{H}$ in the horizon
$H$, so censoring becomes informative whenever $H$ is associated with success.
We give assumption-free identification bounds, then show the complete-case
estimand satisfies $p_{\mathrm{cc}}-p=\mathrm{Cov}(Y,s^{H})/\mathbb{E}[s^{H}]$,
while scoring invalid runs as failures always understates $p$. Two public
corpora --- 80{,}036 software-engineering and 4{,}950 tool-agent trajectories
--- behave exactly as the theory predicts: where successful runs are much
shorter, dropping invalid runs inflates reliability by 2.7 points at a 2\%
hazard; where horizons are nearly outcome-independent, that bias almost
vanishes while invalid-as-failure still costs 12.6 points. Censoring-aware
weighting recovers the estimand when the mechanism is known. We recommend
benchmarks report validity counts and censoring sensitivity rather than silently
choosing a policy.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A long-horizon agent run can end for two very different reasons. The agent can
fail the task, or the measurement apparatus can fail the agent: a provider quota
is exhausted, an API returns 503, a container dies, a harness times out. Only
the first is evidence about the agent. Yet benchmark reports usually collapse
both into one success rate through an unstated policy --- either invalid runs
are scored as failures, or they are deleted.

We are not the first to find validity problems in agent benchmarks
\citep{kapoor2024agentsthatmatter,weidinger2025evalscience,zhu2026guardrails},
and a recent audit finds that agent benchmark papers disclose little about their
harness, inference, and failure handling \citep{moghadasi2026disclose}. Our
claim is narrower: \emph{the two obvious ways of handling invalid runs are
biased in opposite directions, and neither is conservative.} The reason is
structural. Suppose infrastructure failures are entirely independent of what the
agent is doing, arriving with a small constant hazard $q$ per agent decision
step. A run of horizon $H$ then survives with probability $s^{H}$, $s=1-q$.
Long runs are exposed more often, so an outcome-\emph{independent} per-step
failure becomes informative \emph{at the trajectory level} as soon as horizon is
associated with success. On our primary corpus resolved runs average 15.2 agent
steps and unresolved runs 28.7.

\paragraph{Terminology.} We use \emph{censoring} operationally, to denote
premature termination before a semantic endpoint is observed. Statistically this
induces \emph{missing binary outcomes} through a sequential observation process;
we do not claim the task outcome is a conventional time-to-event variable, and
$H$ is a trajectory horizon rather than a clinical event time. We write
$\pi_i$ for an \emph{observation probability}.

\vspace{2pt}
\noindent\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep1pt \parskip0pt \topsep2pt
\item \textbf{Formal.} Assumption-free identification bounds for reliability
under censoring, and a bias identity showing horizon converts
outcome-independent per-step failures into opposite-signed policy bias
(Section~\ref{sec:model}).
\item \textbf{Empirical.} Across two public corpora ($\approx$85k trajectories)
we quantify how each policy distorts aggregate and model-level reliability over
a predeclared hazard grid, including a case where the predicted bias is
\emph{small} (Sections~\ref{sec:setup}--\ref{sec:results}).
\item \textbf{Practice.} Censoring-aware estimation under known and
misspecified mechanisms, and a minimum validity record for benchmark reports
(Section~\ref{sec:practice}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Identification Under Censoring}
\label{sec:model}

Let $Y_i\in\{0,1\}$ be the semantic task outcome, $H_i$ the horizon in agent
decision steps, and $C_i\in\{0,1\}$ the indicator that run $i$ stays valid long
enough for $Y_i$ to be observed. The estimand is $p=\mathbb{E}[Y]$.

\paragraph{Proposition 1 (Assumption-free bounds).}
Let $N$ be initiated runs, $M$ the censored count, and $S$ the observed
successes among uncensored runs. With no assumption whatsoever about the
outcomes of censored runs,
\begin{equation}
S/N \;\le\; p \;\le\; (S+M)/N .
\label{eq:bounds}
\end{equation}
\emph{Proof.} Each censored run contributes an unknown $Y\in\{0,1\}$; setting
all to $0$ gives the lower endpoint and all to $1$ the upper.\hfill$\square$

This is the agent-evaluation instance of a classical worst-case bound
\citep{manski1990nonparametric}, and it reframes one policy immediately:
$S/N$ --- invalid-as-failure --- is not an estimate of reliability but the
\emph{lower endpoint of the identified interval}. Complete-case reporting
$S/(N-M)$ estimates $P(Y=1\mid C=1)$, which equals $p$ only under a
non-informative observation assumption.

\paragraph{Observation model.} We now assume a constant per-step hazard applied
independently of agent behaviour, so that
\begin{equation}
\pi_i \;=\; \Pr(C_i=1\mid H_i)\;=\;s^{H_i},\qquad s=1-q,
\label{eq:pi}
\end{equation}
together with $C\perp Y\mid H$ and $\mathbb{E}[s^{H}]>0$.

\paragraph{Proposition 2 (Horizon-induced complete-case bias).}
Under \eqref{eq:pi}, the complete-case \emph{induced estimand} is
$p_{\mathrm{cc}}=\mathbb{E}[Ys^{H}]/\mathbb{E}[s^{H}]=P(Y{=}1\mid C{=}1)$ and
\begin{equation}
p_{\mathrm{cc}}-p=\frac{\mathrm{Cov}(Y,s^{H})}{\mathbb{E}[s^{H}]} .
\label{eq:cc}
\end{equation}
\emph{Proof.} $\mathbb{E}[YC]=\mathbb{E}[Y\,\mathbb{E}[C\mid Y,H]]=\mathbb{E}[Ys^{H}]$
and $\mathbb{E}[C]=\mathbb{E}[s^{H}]$; subtract $p$ and use
$\mathbb{E}[Ys^{H}]-\mathbb{E}[Y]\mathbb{E}[s^{H}]=\mathrm{Cov}(Y,s^{H})$.\hfill$\square$

Since $s^{H}$ decreases in $H$, the sign of the complete-case bias is the sign
of the outcome--horizon association: if successes are \emph{shorter},
$\mathrm{Cov}(Y,s^{H})>0$ and dropping invalid runs \emph{overstates}
reliability. We stress that $p_{\mathrm{cc}}$ is the induced population
estimand, i.e.\ the probability limit of the complete-case ratio estimator, not
its exact finite-sample expectation --- the expectation of a ratio is not the
ratio of expectations. Our Monte Carlo reports the estimator's realised mean
separately.

\paragraph{Proposition 3 (Invalid-as-failure).}
$\hat p_{\mathrm{fail}}=n^{-1}\sum_i C_iY_i$ is linear in $C$, so it has exact
conditional expectation $n^{-1}\sum_i \pi_i Y_i$, and
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}[\hat p_{\mathrm{fail}}]-p=\mathbb{E}\!\left[Y(s^{H}-1)\right]\le 0 .
\label{eq:fail}
\end{equation}
The bias is at least $-p\,(1-\mathbb{E}[s^{H}])$ in magnitude \emph{regardless}
of any outcome--horizon association, so it scales with the base rate.

\paragraph{Proposition 4 (Weighted identification).}
If $\pi_i>0$ is known or consistently estimable, the Horvitz--Thompson
estimator $\hat p_{\mathrm{HT}}=n^{-1}\sum_i C_iY_i/\pi_i$ is exactly unbiased
conditional on the finite population, since $\mathbb{E}[C_i/\pi_i]=1$
\citep{horvitz1952generalization,robins1994estimation}. The H\'ajek form
$\hat p_{\mathrm{H}}=\sum_i C_iY_i/\pi_i\big/\sum_i C_i/\pi_i$ is a ratio
estimator: consistent and approximately unbiased, not exactly unbiased. Both
degrade as $\pi_i\to0$, a positivity problem weighting cannot fix.

\paragraph{Three levels of reporting.} Together these give a ladder.
\emph{(L1) No assumptions:} report \eqref{eq:bounds}.
\emph{(L2) Non-informative observation:} the complete-case reading may be
justified. \emph{(L3) Modelled horizon-dependent observation:} weight or run a
sensitivity analysis. IPCW does not solve arbitrary unknown or
outcome-dependent censoring, and we do not claim it does.

\section{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Corpora.} \emph{Primary:} 80{,}036 SWE-agent
\citep{yang2024sweagent} trajectories \citep{nebius2025trajectories}
(CC-BY-4.0) on SWE-bench-style tasks
\citep{jimenez2024swebench,openai2024swebenchverified}, each with a
ground-truth resolution label from test execution; we read five pruned columns
($\approx$0.6\,MB of a 1.11\,GB corpus).
\emph{Secondary:} 4{,}950 $\tau$-bench trajectories
\citep{agentsuite2026taubench,yao2024taubench} over 29 model identifiers and
165 tasks each, with strictly binary \texttt{score}. We retain every labelled
row. In the SWE corpus one row has $H=0$ (context exhausted before the agent
acted); it is kept, since $\pi=s^{0}=1$ makes it never censored. We do not
filter on the datasets' own exit statuses, which are associated with success
(resolve rate 0.248 for \texttt{submitted} vs.\ 0.000 for
\texttt{exit\_context}) and are part of the historical generating process, not
our censoring.

\paragraph{Horizon.} $H$ counts agent decision turns only --- \texttt{role="ai"}
in the SWE corpus, \texttt{role="assistant"} in $\tau$-bench --- excluding
system, simulated-user, and tool turns. Both definitions were fixed before any
censoring result. We re-fetched 20 full trajectories per corpus (seed 20260905)
and recounted independently: \textbf{20/20 exact agreement in both}. Our SWE
counts are deliberately about half the dataset card's, which counts environment
turns too; $2H{+}1$ reproduces the card.

\paragraph{Censoring is semi-synthetic; results are a finite-corpus sensitivity
analysis.} We inject censoring onto completed, labelled corpora. The grid
$q\in\{0,0.10,0.25,0.50,1.00,2.00\}\%$ per agent step was predeclared and is a
\textbf{sensitivity analysis, not a measured outage rate}. Because these corpora
contain repeated tasks and unequal model coverage, we do not treat rows as
i.i.d.\ draws from a universal agent population: our quantities describe what
would happen \emph{to these corpora} under declared mechanisms, and Monte Carlo
intervals (200 replicates, seeds from 20260905) reflect injected censoring
randomness only, not sampling uncertainty about a wider population.

\paragraph{The two corpora dissociate the two biases.}
Table~\ref{tab:cross} and Figure~\ref{fig:main} are the main result. On
SWE-agent, successes are far shorter (15.2 vs.\ 28.7 steps), so
$\mathrm{Cov}(Y,s^{H})>0$ and complete-case reporting inflates reliability:
$+0.84$ points at $q=0.5\%$ and $+2.69$ at $q=2\%$, against $-1.20$ and $-4.12$
for invalid-as-failure --- a 6.8-point spread between two defensible policies on
identical data. On $\tau$-bench the horizon gap is only 1.1 steps, and exactly
as \eqref{eq:cc} predicts the complete-case bias nearly vanishes ($+0.40$ points
at $q=2\%$). Yet invalid-as-failure is \emph{worse} there ($-12.64$ points),
because by \eqref{eq:fail} its bias scales with the base rate, which is
55.0\%. This is the prediction of the theory, not a reproduction of the first
corpus: the complete-case bias follows the outcome--horizon association, and the
invalid-as-failure bias does not.

\paragraph{Bounds are wide.} At $q=1\%$, \eqref{eq:bounds} gives
$[14.5,35.3]$ for SWE-agent and $[48.3,60.9]$ for $\tau$-bench. Reporting a
single number without stating the mechanism is a strong assumption, not a
neutral default.

\paragraph{The mechanism is visible in horizon strata.} On SWE-agent, runs of
1--25 steps are 65.3\% of the corpus and succeed at 23.0\%; runs beyond 75 steps
are 4.4\% and succeed at 2.6\%. At $q=2\%$ the short stratum retains 77.6\% of
runs, the long stratum 10.5\%. Censoring preferentially deletes long,
low-success runs, which is why survivors look better.

\paragraph{IPCW recovers the estimand when the mechanism is known.}
Horvitz--Thompson bias never exceeds $0.005$ points (SWE) or $0.017$ points
($\tau$-bench) at any hazard. The cost is variance: HT standard deviation grows
from $0.016$ to $0.096$ points (SWE) as $q$ goes $0.1\%\!\to\!2\%$. Under a
misspecified mechanism --- censoring with a hazard that doubles beyond step 25,
weighting as if constant --- HT bias moves to $-0.14$ ($q_0=0.5\%$) and $-0.26$
points ($q_0=1\%$), and H\'ajek to $+0.55$ and $+0.97$. Correction degrades
gracefully here, but it does degrade.

\paragraph{Ranking distortion is real but rare.}
With 29 eligible $\tau$-bench models ($\geq$100 trajectories; 406 pairs, average
ranks for ties) the first pairwise reversal appears at $q=0.5\%$ under both
policies. At $q=2\%$ complete-case produces 2 reversals ($\rho=0.998$,
$\tau_b=0.984$) and invalid-as-failure 11 ($\rho=0.990$, $\tau_b=0.939$). On
SWE-agent, with only 3 eligible models, we observed \textbf{zero} reversals at
every hazard --- a weak test we report as such. Ordering is fairly robust;
\emph{margins} are not, and comparative claims about gaps are the vulnerable
ones.

\paragraph{Task balancing does not change the conclusion.}
Weighting each benchmark task equally rather than each trajectory, the SWE base
rate falls from 16.73\% to 9.80\% but the $q=1\%$ biases keep their sign and
order of magnitude ($+1.14$/$-1.30$ points); on $\tau$-bench the base rate moves
55.0\%$\to$56.9\% with biases $+0.22$/$-7.29$. The directional result is not an
artifact of unequal task or model coverage.

\section{From Estimand to Practice}
\label{sec:practice}

Infrastructure invalidation is not hypothetical. In our own instrumented runs,
two provider-backed agent executions completed 9 and 4 agent steps before
rate-limit invalidation, and six continuations restored from checkpoints were
invalidated before executing any step; no valid outcome existed for any of the
eight, and our harness assigned none. We report this only as an existence
demonstration --- it is not a provider reliability estimate, and its frequency
is \emph{not} used as $q$ anywhere above.

\paragraph{Minimum validity record.} We suggest a benchmark report state, at
minimum: (1) initiated runs; (2) runs reaching valid semantic evaluation;
(3) invalid/censored counts by reason; (4) the horizon or resource-at-risk
distribution; (5) the policy applied to censored outcomes; and (6) sensitivity
of the headline number to plausible censoring mechanisms. When the mechanism is
not defensibly known, report bounds or a sensitivity range rather than silently
deleting runs or scoring them as failures. Recording this is cheap; our artifact
enforces the underlying separations --- online features never see the outcome,
provider events never increment semantic failure counters, and
infrastructure-invalid runs are structurally refused a label --- as regression
tests.

\section{Related Work}

Where prior work studies how agents fail, we study which executions may be
counted as evidence. \citet{zhao2026failure} anatomise coding-agent failure over
complete trajectories; \citet{ruan2026doomed} abort doomed episodes early, an
intervention that is itself a censoring mechanism under our framing and whose
hazard would need reporting; \citet{li2026early} derive online warning signals
and encounter, but do not model, a missing-outcome population.
\citet{gonuguntla2026replay} show static replay under a substituted model scores
a counterfactual world --- procedure validity rather than missing outcomes. Two
papers use adjacent vocabulary: \citet{wang2026interface} use ``censoring'' for
interface-level output suppression, with no missing-data model, and
\citet{raghu2026proper} extend proper scoring rules to administratively censored
trajectories, the closest work, though it scores predictions rather than
identifying an aggregate benchmark estimand and uses no inverse-probability
weighting. On validity and disclosure more broadly,
\citet{kapoor2024agentsthatmatter}, \citet{weidinger2025evalscience},
\citet{zhu2026guardrails}, \citet{chen2026judge} and \citet{moghadasi2026disclose}
are complementary: disclosure tells readers what happened, whereas we study how
missing semantic outcomes change the estimand itself. Our estimators are
classical \citep{horvitz1952generalization,robins1994estimation,manski1990nonparametric};
the contribution is the mapping onto agent benchmarks, the horizon-covariance
result, and its magnitude on real trajectories. We do not claim to be first.

\section{Limitations}

(1) The censoring study is \textbf{semi-synthetic}: we inject censoring rather
than observe outages. (2) The $q$ grid is a sensitivity analysis, not a measured
rate. (3) The model assumes independent constant per-step censoring with
$C\perp Y\mid H$; the stress test probes only one departure, and genuinely
outcome-dependent (MNAR) censoring is out of scope. (4) IPCW needs a correct,
estimable mechanism, which real harnesses must fit rather than know.
(5) Positivity and variance degrade for long horizons and high $q$. (6) Both
corpora contain repeated tasks and unequal model coverage, so results are
finite-corpus statements, not population inference; one $\tau$-bench identifier
covers 330 rows because two files share a \texttt{model\_path}. (7) The SWE
ranking analysis has only 3 eligible models and is a weak test. (8) The real
provider case shows existence, not prevalence. (9) Replication on other domains,
scaffolds, and genuinely observed infrastructure failures remains future work.

\section{Conclusion}

An invalid execution is not a failed one. Scoring it as failure and deleting it
are both biased, in opposite directions, and the bias survives even when
infrastructure failures are independent of the agent, because horizon compounds
exposure. Two corpora behave exactly as the identity predicts: where successes
are much shorter, deletion inflates reliability; where horizons are nearly
outcome-independent, deletion is nearly safe while scoring-as-failure is
worst. The practical implication is modest and concrete: record validity per
run, report the censoring mechanism, and when it is unknown, report bounds
instead of a number.
